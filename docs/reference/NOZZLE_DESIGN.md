## Part One — The Published Literature

Every item below is a real, published, citable source. I have grouped them by what each one is actually useful for, because a list of forty papers with no guidance is worse than a list of twelve you will actually read.

### Group A — The contour itself (read these first)

**Rao, G. V. R. (1958). "Exhaust Nozzle Contour for Optimum Thrust." *Journal of Jet Propulsion*, volume 28, number 6, pages 377 to 382. Digital object identifier 10.2514/8.7324.**

This is the origin of the bell nozzle. Rao applied the calculus of variations to find the wall shape that produces maximum thrust for a given length and expansion area ratio. Every bell nozzle flying today descends from this paper. Rao worked at Marquardt Aircraft when he wrote it, then moved to Rocketdyne. George Sutton, who worked alongside him, later wrote that the curved exit contour has been used almost universally for nozzles designed since about 1960, for both solid and liquid propellants.

**Rao, G. V. R. (1960). "Approximation of Optimum Thrust Nozzle Contour." *American Rocket Society Journal*, volume 30, number 6, page 561.**

This is the paper you will actually use. The full calculus of variations solution is laborious. Here Rao shows that a simple parabola through the same end points, with the same end wall angles, reproduces the optimum contour to within a fraction of one percent of thrust. This approximation is what our design script implements.

**Rao, G. V. R. (1961). "Recent Developments in Rocket Nozzle Configurations." *American Rocket Society Journal*, volume 31, number 11, pages 1488 to 1494.**

Contains the wall angle charts. You read the initial parabola angle and the exit parabola angle off these charts as functions of expansion area ratio and fractional length. Reproduced in nearly every propulsion textbook, including Sutton and Biblarz figure 3-14.

### Group B — Design practice and dimensioning

**Ellis, R. A. (1975). "Solid Rocket Motor Nozzles." NASA Space Vehicle Design Criteria (Chemical Propulsion), NASA Special Publication 8115.**

The most useful single document in this entire list. It is a distillation of what the American solid rocket industry had learned by 1975, organised as design criteria and recommended practice rather than as theory. It covers the entrance region, the throat region, the exit cone, throat inserts, thermal liners, insulator materials, and structural attachment. It is also candid about recurring failure modes: graphite cracking and ejection, differential erosion where two materials meet, inadequate non-destructive testing, and uncertainty in adhesive bonding. Every one of those is a live risk for a student-built nozzle.

Specific practices taken from this document into our design: the convergent inlet half angle ranges from one to seventy five degrees with forty five degrees being standard; steeper inlets increase insulation erosion, especially at higher chamber pressures; the cylindrical land at the throat must be at least half the throat radius long, to aid alignment, machining and erosion tolerance; and a contoured divergent section delivers a half to one percent improvement in delivered specific impulse over a conical one.

**Balmogim, U. (2017). *Design and Development of the Phoenix-1B Hybrid Rocket*. Master of Science thesis, University of KwaZulu-Natal. Chapter 5.**

Already in your project folder, and the single closest analogue to what you are building: a paraffin and nitrous oxide hybrid sounding rocket. Chapter 5 works the whole nozzle problem end to end, including the Rao parabolic approximation, the Bartz heat transfer analysis, and a coupled transient thermal and structural analysis. The final Phoenix-1B nozzle had a throat diameter of thirty two millimetres, an expansion area ratio of 6.38, and an overall length of one hundred and fifty six millimetres, with a steel casing supporting silica phenolic insulation and a graphite throat insert.

Chapter 2 is equally important for a different reason. The predecessor vehicle, Phoenix-1A, lost its nozzle in flight. High speed footage showed the graphite divergent section being ejected from the combustion chamber shortly after ignition, after which the boat tail was lost. The cause was attributed to a hard start combined with a stress concentration between the nozzle and its retaining ring. An intentional gap had been left for thermal expansion, but graphite is much stronger in compression than in tension, and the graphite was loaded in tension at the stress concentration. **Read this before you design your retention scheme.**

**Sutton, G. P., and Biblarz, O. *Rocket Propulsion Elements*, ninth edition, John Wiley and Sons, 2016. Chapter 3.**

Not a paper, but the standard reference for the one-dimensional relations, the thrust coefficient, divergence loss factors, and the reproduced Rao wall angle charts.

### Group C — Heat transfer

**Bartz, D. R. (1957). "A Simple Equation for Rapid Estimation of Rocket Nozzle Convective Heat Transfer Coefficients." *Journal of Jet Propulsion*, volume 27, number 1, pages 49 to 51. Digital object identifier 10.2514/8.12572.**

Three pages, published almost seventy years ago, still the first thing every nozzle designer computes. It gives the convective heat transfer coefficient along the wall from quantities you already have out of the chemistry code. Our script implements it directly.

**Bartz, D. R. (1965). "Turbulent Boundary-Layer Heat Transfer from Rapidly Accelerating Flow of Rocket Combustion Gases and of Heated Air." *Advances in Heat Transfer*, volume 2, pages 1 to 108.**

The long version, explaining where the 1957 correlation comes from and where it breaks down. Read this if you intend to defend your heat transfer numbers to an examiner.

**Smith, D. M. (1970). *A Comparison of Experimental Heat-Transfer Coefficients in a Nozzle with Analytical Predictions from Bartz's Methods for Various Combustion Chamber Pressures in a Solid Rocket Motor*. North Carolina State University.**

Measured against predicted. Useful for setting an honest error bar on the Bartz result.

**Mayer, E. (1961). "Analysis of Convective Heat Transfer in Rocket Nozzles." *American Rocket Society Journal*, volume 31, July, pages 911 to 917. Digital object identifier 10.2514/8.5675.**

An independent method. Running two correlations and comparing is a cheap sanity check.

### Group D — Erosion, which is the hybrid-specific problem

Nozzle erosion is worse and less predictable in a hybrid than in a solid motor, for two reasons: the combustion products contain a higher concentration of oxidising species, and the mixture ratio shifts during the burn. This group matters more for you than it would for a solid motor team.

**Bianchi, D., Migliorino, M. T., and Nasuti, F. "Computational Analysis and Regression Laws for Nozzle Erosion Prediction in Hybrid Rockets." *Journal of Propulsion and Power*. Digital object identifier 10.2514/1.B39322.**

The most directly applicable paper in this group. It studies graphite nozzle erosion in hybrid rocket engines burning high density polyethylene with two oxidisers, oxygen **and nitrous oxide**. It derives closed-form regression laws that let you estimate throat erosion and wall temperature from chamber pressure and mixture ratio without running a full computational fluid dynamics case, then couples those laws to a one-dimensional transient heat conduction solver and validates against experiment. It also shows how gas-phase reactions change the surface chemistry as you move from fuel-rich to oxidiser-rich conditions, which is exactly the transition your motor makes.

**Migliorino, M. T., Bianchi, D., and Nasuti, F. (2021). "Graphite Nozzle Erosion Trends in Paraffin/Oxygen Hybrid Rockets." *Journal of Propulsion and Power*. Digital object identifier 10.2514/1.B38574.**

Paraffin specifically. Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes simulations of the internal ballistics coupled to graphite ablation. The important practical finding for you: erosion interacts strongly with the radially non-uniform flow field that comes out of incomplete mixing, and lengthening the post-combustion chamber improves combustion efficiency but changes the erosion picture. This is a direct argument for taking your post-combustion chamber design seriously.

**Zhang, X., Wang, Z., Wang, R., Lu, C., Yu, R., and Tian, H. (2022). "Numerical simulation of chemical ablation and mechanical erosion in hybrid rocket nozzle." *Acta Astronautica*, volume 192, pages 82 to 96. Digital object identifier 10.1016/j.actaastro.2021.12.012.**

Separates chemical ablation from mechanical erosion, which are different mechanisms with different remedies.

**Thakre, P., and Yang, V. (2008). "Chemical Erosion of Carbon-Carbon/Graphite Nozzles in Solid-Propellant Rocket Motors." *Journal of Propulsion and Power*, volume 24, number 4, pages 822 to 833.**

The reference treatment of the surface chemistry. Cited by the Phoenix thesis for the physicochemical processes that drive erosion.

**Kamps, L. T., et al. Work on oxidiser-to-fuel-ratio control and throat erosion in hybrid rockets.** Reports that controlling the mixture ratio produced a throat erosion improvement worth about 5.75 percent of the total specific impulse increase at small scale, falling to 1.21 percent as scale increased. Useful for deciding how much effort erosion control deserves at your size.

### Group E — Related and worth knowing about

**Hoffman, J. D. (1987). "Design of Compressed Truncated Perfect Nozzles." *Journal of Propulsion and Power*, volume 3, number 2, pages 150 to 156.** An alternative to the Rao method.

**Sternin, L. (2000). "Analysis of the thrust characteristics of jet nozzles designed by various methods." *Fluid Dynamics*, volume 35, number 1, pages 123 to 131.** Compares what the different contour methods actually deliver.

**Davis, D. (1982). "Investigation of optimization techniques for solid rocket motor nozzle contour." American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics paper 82-1188.**

**Cross, P. G., and Boyd, I. D. (2019). "Conjugate Analyses of Ablation in Rocket Nozzles." *Journal of Spacecraft and Rockets*, volume 56, number 5, pages 1593 to 1610.** If you eventually do coupled flow and thermal computational fluid dynamics with an ablating wall.

**Gordon, S., and McBride, B. J. (1994, 1996). *Computer Program for Calculation of Complex Chemical Equilibrium Compositions and Applications*. NASA Reference Publication 1311, parts one and two.** Where the chamber conditions used below come from.

## Part Two — Deriving the Nozzle

### Where the inputs come from

Nothing in the nozzle design is invented. Every input traces back to something already established in this project.

| Input | Value | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|

| Oxidizer to fuel ratio by mass | 6.5 | Chosen so the burn sweeps downward through the peak characteristic velocity point at 7.0 |
| Total mass flow rate | 2.868 kilograms per second | Baseline motor design, for an average thrust near 5,150 newtons |
| Expansion area ratio | 6.0 | Compromise between sea level performance and the low ambient pressure near apogee |
| Case inner diameter | 118 millimetres | Fits the 113 millimetre grain outer diameter inside a five inch airframe |
| Burn duration | 8.42 seconds | Baseline motor design |
| Characteristic velocity efficiency | 0.90 | Conservative value for a well designed paraffin motor with a post-combustion chamber |

|---|---|

| Characteristic velocity, delivered at 0.90 efficiency | 1,435.6 metres per second |

### Step one, the throat area

The throat area follows from the definition of characteristic velocity:

> throat area = (total mass flow rate × delivered characteristic velocity) ÷ chamber pressure

> throat area = (2.868 × 1435.6) ÷ 3,500,000 = 1,176.4 square millimetres

giving a **throat diameter of 38.70 millimetres**.

Note that this uses the *delivered* characteristic velocity, not the ideal one. If you size the throat on the ideal value, your real chamber pressure will come out ten percent low and every downstream number will be wrong.

### Step two, the exit area

> exit area = expansion area ratio × throat area = 6.0 × 1,176.4 = 7,058.4 square millimetres

giving an **exit diameter of 94.80 millimetres**. This fits comfortably inside a 127 millimetre airframe with room for a boat tail.

### Step three, the convergent section

Following NASA Special Publication 8115:

- The **contraction area ratio** is set by the case, not chosen freely. With a 118 millimetre case inner diameter and a 38.70 millimetre throat, it is **9.30**. The Phoenix-1B used 8. Anything above about 4 makes the chamber velocity negligible, so this is comfortable.
- The **convergence half angle** is **45 degrees**, the standard value. Steeper angles increase insulation erosion.
- A circular arc of radius 1.5 times the throat radius blends the chamber wall into the convergent cone.

### Step four, the throat region

- **Upstream throat arc radius** = 1.5 × throat radius = **29.03 millimetres**. This is the standard ratio and it also sets the radius of curvature used in the Bartz correlation.
- **Downstream throat arc radius** = 0.382 × throat radius = **7.39 millimetres**. This ratio comes from Rao and is essentially universal.
- **Cylindrical throat land length** = 0.5 × throat radius = **9.68 millimetres**. NASA Special Publication 8115 requires at least half the throat radius. A parallel land makes the throat far easier to machine and to measure, helps with alignment, and means that the first fraction of a millimetre of erosion does not immediately change the throat area.

### Step five, the divergent bell

The reference length is a 15 degree half angle cone:

> reference conical length = [(exit radius − throat radius) + downstream arc radius × (sec 15° − 1)] ÷ tan 15°
> = **105.65 millimetres**

We use a **fractional length of 1.00**, meaning the bell is the same length as that reference cone. The bell is not shorter, but it is more efficient, because the flow leaves nearly axially instead of at 15 degrees.

The wall angles come from the Rao charts at this expansion area ratio and fractional length:

- **Initial parabola wall angle: 20.75 degrees**
- **Exit parabola wall angle: 7.50 degrees**

These are the values the Phoenix-1B used at an expansion area ratio of 6.25, which is close enough to our 6.0 to adopt directly.

The parabola itself is generated as a quadratic Bézier curve through the start point at the end of the downstream throat arc and the exit point, with those two tangent angles. That curve is mathematically the same conic Rao's approximation describes, and it is far better behaved numerically than solving for the four parabola coefficients directly.

**Why a bell rather than a cone.** A 15 degree cone loses about 1.7 percent of its axial thrust to flow divergence at the exit. A full length bell recovers most of that. NASA Special Publication 8115 puts the gain at half to one percent of delivered specific impulse. On a 43,000 newton second motor that is roughly 300 newton seconds, which is not nothing. The cost is that you have to machine a curve instead of a straight taper.

### Flow conditions along the wall

| Station | Mach number | Static pressure | Static temperature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chamber entrance | 0.07 | 34.9 bar | 3,220 kelvin |
| Throat | 1.00 | 20.1 bar | 2,967 kelvin |
| Exit | 2.85 | 0.85 bar | 1,613 kelvin |

The exit static pressure of 0.85 bar against a sea level ambient of 1.013 bar means the nozzle is **slightly overexpanded at liftoff**. The pressure ratio is 0.84, comfortably above the roughly 0.35 threshold where flow separation becomes a concern, so this is safe. It becomes correctly expanded at around 1,300 metres and underexpanded above that, which is the right compromise for a flight that spends most of its burn climbing.

### Heat transfer, from the Bartz correlation

Assuming a graphite wall running at 2,500 kelvin:

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Adiabatic wall temperature at throat | 3,167 kelvin |
| Heat transfer coefficient at throat | 5,209 watts per square metre kelvin |
| Heat flux at throat | 3.47 megawatts per square metre |
| Peak heat flux | 3.52 megawatts per square metre |
| Location of peak | 2.6 millimetres upstream of the throat |
| Integrated heat rate into the wall | 56.1 kilowatts |
| Total heat load over the 8.42 second burn | 0.47 megajoules |

Two things to take from this.

**The peak is upstream of the throat, not at it.** This is a standard and slightly counterintuitive result of the Bartz correlation, and it is why throat inserts are made long enough to extend into the convergent section. If you size your insert to cover only the geometric throat, the hottest part of the nozzle will be sitting on your insulation instead.

**Three and a half megawatts per square metre is a serious flux.** For comparison it is roughly the heat flux on the throat of a small liquid engine. Nothing you can make will survive it indefinitely. The design strategy is not to resist it but to absorb it for 8.4 seconds and then stop, which is what a thick graphite insert with a phenolic backing does.

### Throat erosion allowance

Graphite recession rates in hybrid rockets with nitrous oxide span roughly 0.03 to 0.15 millimetres per second in the published literature, depending strongly on chamber pressure and mixture ratio. Taking a bracket of 0.05 to 0.10:

| | Low estimate, 0.05 mm/s | High estimate, 0.10 mm/s |
|---|---|---|
| Radial recession over the burn | 0.42 millimetres | 0.84 millimetres |
| Final throat diameter | 39.54 millimetres | 40.39 millimetres |
| Throat area growth | 4.4 percent | 8.9 percent |
| Chamber pressure at end of burn | 33.5 bar | 32.1 bar |
| Effective expansion area ratio at end of burn | 5.75 | 5.51 |

**This is a real effect and you must account for it.** A nine percent throat area growth drops your chamber pressure by nine percent, which drops thrust, which changes your trajectory. It also reduces the effective expansion area ratio, moving the nozzle further from optimal.

The practical consequences:

1. **Measure the throat before and after every static fire.** A bore gauge and five minutes. This single measurement gives you your actual recession rate, which is worth more than any published correlation.
2. **Consider sizing the throat 2 to 3 percent under nominal** so that the burn-averaged throat area is closer to the design value, rather than starting at design and drifting above it the whole time.
3. **Your motor runs oxidiser-rich compared with a solid**, at a mixture ratio of 6.5 with roughly half the exhaust being nitrogen and a meaningful concentration of carbon dioxide, water vapour and hydroxyl radicals. All three attack carbon. Expect worse erosion than a solid motor team would quote you.

### Materials and construction

This follows the Phoenix-1B arrangement, which was itself derived from NASA Special Publication 8115 and which survived flight after its predecessor's nozzle did not.

| Component | Material | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Throat insert | Polycrystalline graphite, fine grain | Takes the peak heat flux and the erosion |
| Convergent liner | Silica phenolic | Insulates the structure through the convergent section |
| Divergent liner | Silica phenolic | Forms the bell contour, insulates, ablates slowly |
| Structural shell | Steel or aluminium | Carries pressure and thrust loads, bolts to the case |
| Retaining ring | Steel | Holds the insert in axial compression |
| Sealing | O-rings at the case interface | Prevents hot gas leakage past the joint |

**The single most important design rule here, learned the hard way by the Phoenix team: graphite is much stronger in compression than in tension, so the retention scheme must load the insert in compression at all times.** Phoenix-1A left an intentional expansion gap between the graphite and the steel retainer, a stress concentration developed at that interface, the graphite was loaded in tension, and the divergent section was ejected from the motor shortly after ignition. The boat tail went with it and the vehicle reached 2.5 kilometres instead of 10.

Design the retention so that thermal expansion increases the compressive preload rather than opening a gap. Graphite has a low coefficient of thermal expansion and steel has a high one, so a steel ring around a graphite insert will loosen as it heats. Account for that.

Two further practical points from NASA Special Publication 8115: differential erosion at material interfaces is a recurring failure mode, so put your material joints where the heat flux is low, not near the throat; and adhesive bonding is a persistent source of uncertainty, so do not make a bond line the only thing holding a component in place.

### Things this design does not yet include

Be honest about these in your reviews.

- **No transient thermal analysis of the solid.** The heat flux calculated here is the gas-side boundary condition. What you still need is a transient conduction analysis through the graphite and phenolic to confirm that the structural shell stays below its temperature limit for 8.4 seconds. That is a finite element problem, and it is what Chapter 5 of the Phoenix thesis does.
- **No structural analysis.** Pressure loads, thrust loads, bolt pattern, and the thermal stress in the graphite are all outstanding.
- **No radiation.** In a sooty paraffin flame, radiation contributes meaningfully to the wall heat flux. The Bartz correlation is convection only, so treat the numbers here as a lower bound.
- **No allowance for a non-uniform inlet.** The Migliorino paper shows that incomplete mixing produces a radially non-uniform flow entering the nozzle, and that this changes the erosion distribution. A good post-combustion chamber reduces this.
- **The wall temperature of 2,500 kelvin is assumed, not solved.** Heat flux is sensitive to it. Once you have the transient conduction solution, come back and iterate.

### Step 4 — Get the contour into computer aided design software

This is the point of the whole exercise. The file `nozzle_contour.csv` has one row per point along the wall, with the axial position and wall radius in millimetres in the first two columns.

**For Fusion 360:** Insert menu, then Insert Mesh or the "Import Spline CSV" add-in. You want a two-column file of axial position and radius, so first strip the extra columns. In the terminal:

```
python -c "
import csv
rows = list(csv.reader(open('outputs/nozzle_contour.csv')))[1:]
with open('outputs/contour_for_cad.csv','w',newline='') as f:
    w = csv.writer(f)
    for r in rows:
        w.writerow([r[0], r[1], 0])
print('wrote outputs/contour_for_cad.csv')
"
```

That writes an axial, radial, zero triple per line, which is the format most spline importers expect. Import it as a sketch on a plane containing the axis, then use **Revolve** about the axis to get the solid.

**For SolidWorks:** Insert, Curve, Curve Through XYZ Points. It wants a text file of three columns separated by tabs or spaces. Same idea, change the writer to use tab delimiters.

**For FreeCAD, which is free:** the Curves workbench has a "Points to spline" tool that reads comma separated values directly.

Once revolved, you have the flow surface. The physical part is that surface plus wall thickness, plus the throat insert split line, plus your bolt flange.

### Step 5 — Change something and see what happens

This is how you learn what the design is sensitive to.

Open `run_nozzle.py`. Near the top is a block of design point constants. Try changing the expansion area ratio:

```python
EXPANSION_AREA_RATIO = 8.0        # was 6.0
```

Save with **Control and S**, then run again. The exit diameter grows to about 109 millimetres and the divergent section gets longer. Ask yourself whether that still fits your airframe.

Then try a shorter bell, which is what most flight nozzles actually use:

```python
    contour = nozzle.build_nozzle_contour(
        throat_radius=throat_radius,
        expansion_area_ratio=EXPANSION_AREA_RATIO,
        chamber_radius=chamber_radius,
        parabola_initial_angle_degrees=22.5,     # read off the Rao chart
        parabola_exit_angle_degrees=14.0,        # for 80 percent length
        fractional_length=0.80)                  # was 1.00
```

That gets you a nozzle about twenty percent shorter and lighter for a very small performance penalty. **Important:** if you change the fractional length you must also change both wall angles, because they are read off the Rao chart together. Leaving the full-length angles on an eighty percent bell gives you a contour that is not optimal and may not even be smooth.

Other constants worth changing: `ASSUMED_WALL_TEMPERATURE` to see how sensitive the heat flux is, `CHAMBER_PRESSURE_BAR` and `TOTAL_MASS_FLOW_RATE` if you revise the motor, and the two recession rate estimates once you have measured your own.

### Step 6 — Use the functions in your own script

`nozzle_design.py` is a library. You can import it and drive it yourself. Make a new file called `my_study.py` in the `analysis` folder:

```python
import numpy as np
import nozzle_design as nozzle

# How does divergent length trade against expansion area ratio?
for expansion_area_ratio in (4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 7.0, 8.0, 10.0):
    contour = nozzle.build_nozzle_contour(
        throat_radius=0.019351,
        expansion_area_ratio=expansion_area_ratio,
        chamber_radius=0.059,
        parabola_initial_angle_degrees=20.75,
        parabola_exit_angle_degrees=7.50,
        fractional_length=1.00)
    print(f'expansion area ratio {expansion_area_ratio:5.1f}   '
          f'exit diameter {contour["exit_radius"] * 2000:6.2f} mm   '
          f'divergent length {contour["divergent_length"] * 1000:6.2f} mm')
```

Run it with `python my_study.py`. That is the whole workflow: import the library, loop over the thing you care about, print a table.

| `No module named rocketcea` | Environment not activated | Look for `(.venv)` in your prompt |

| Contour looks kinked in the drawing | Wall angles inconsistent with fractional length | Read both angles off the Rao chart for the length you chose |
| `brentq` raises a sign error | Expansion area ratio below 1, or a bad throat radius | Check your inputs are in metres, not millimetres |
